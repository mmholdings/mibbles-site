Bringing home your first cat is exciting and slightly daunting. The cat is small. The cat is uncertain. You are about to get a lot of advice from the internet, much of it conflicting.

Here's a clean version. The first 30 days, organized around what matters at each stage.

<Callout type="quick-answer">
**First-time cat owner essentials:** prepare a quiet starter room before pickup, do a vet wellness exam in the first 1–2 weeks, build a consistent feeding and play routine, introduce changes slowly, and focus on letting your cat earn their confidence. Don't over-handle in the first week — let them set the pace.
</Callout>

## Before pickup (1–2 days ahead)

You need less than you think.

**Buy:**

- Litter box (one for one cat; one per cat + one extra for multi-cat households)
- Unscented clumping litter (most cats prefer it)
- Food and water bowls (ceramic or stainless — plastic harbors bacteria)
- The same food they were eating at the shelter or breeder (sudden food changes cause GI upset)
- A carrier — soft-sided or hard, doesn't matter much
- A scratching post (vertical, sisal, tall enough for a full stretch)
- A simple hiding spot — covered cat bed, cardboard box, or a small open carrier
- 2–3 toys, including at least one wand toy
- A nail trimmer

**Set up the starter room** before the cat arrives. One small, quiet space (bedroom, office). Litter box on one side of the room, food and water on the other (cats prefer separation). Hiding spots available. Curtains slightly open if there's a window. Keep doors closed.

## Day 0: pickup and arrival

- Use the carrier. Don't carry a stressed new cat in your arms.
- Drive home calmly. Put the carrier on the seat, not in the trunk.
- At home, take the carrier directly to the starter room. Open the door and walk away. Don't pull the cat out. Let them emerge when they're ready.
- Sit quietly nearby for 10 minutes, then leave them alone.

That's it. Day 0 is just "get them home and let them be."

## Days 1–7: the starter room

Most cats spend the first few days hiding. Normal. Don't worry. Don't drag them out.

**Each day:**

- Refresh food and water in the morning. Note whether they're eating and drinking (most start within 24 hours; some take 48–72).
- Scoop the litter box. Note that they're using it. (If a cat isn't using the box within 24 hours, that's a vet conversation.)
- Sit in the room for 15–20 minutes, doing your own quiet thing. Read, work on a laptop. Don't stare at the cat. Let them get used to your presence.
- Offer a wand toy from across the room. Don't push. If they don't engage, try tomorrow.

**Vet appointment:** book the first wellness exam for sometime in the first 1–2 weeks. Earlier if anything seems off.

**Signs they're ready to expand their territory:** moving confidently around the starter room, eating well, exploring during the day, approaching you instead of just tolerating you.

## Days 8–14: territory expansion

Open the door. Let them explore at their own pace. Don't force it.

**Goals for this week:**

- Vet visit (if not already done)
- Established feeding routine (2–4 small meals at consistent times)
- First real interactive play session — 5–10 minutes with a wand toy
- Identified favorite resting spots (cat trees, sunbeams, the warm spot by the radiator)
- Litter box habits stable

**Common issues:**

- *They hide in the new spaces.* Normal. Just leave food and water accessible.
- *They have a regression and start hiding more.* Often a sign of overwhelm. Reduce stimulation; let them retreat to the starter room if they want.
- *They start vocalizing more.* Often a good sign — they're starting to communicate.

## Days 15–30: building the routine

This is where the long-term habits start to matter.

### Feeding

Settle on 2–4 scheduled meals at consistent times. Avoid free-feeding if you can — meals are one of the easiest sources of enrichment. By the end of the month, consider adding a food puzzle for at least one meal a day.

### Play

Two short play sessions per day (5–15 minutes each), ideally at dawn and dusk to match natural cat activity. Wand toy. Real prey movement. Let them catch the toy. End with a small meal — completes the natural sequence.

### Quiet time

Cats need predictable quiet time. Same nap spots, undisturbed when sleeping deeply.

### Affection on their terms

Most cats love attention — but on their schedule, in their preferred contact zones (head, chin, cheeks for most; back and sides for many; belly almost never). Pay attention to what your specific cat enjoys. Watch for tail twitching, ear flattening, or skin rippling — those are stop signals.

### Enrichment

Add small enrichment elements as your cat shows they want more:

- Window perch with a bird-feeder view
- A second cat tree in a different spot
- New scents weekly (catnip, silvervine, valerian, fresh herbs)
- Food puzzles of escalating difficulty
- Cat TV sessions during quiet daytime hours

### Health watch

Note your cat's normal: how much they eat and drink, how often they use the litter box, how they greet you, what their energy level is. The most useful thing you can do as a new cat owner is establish your cat's baseline so you'll notice when something shifts.

## What to skip in the first 30 days

- **Bathing.** Cats groom themselves. Bathing is rarely needed and stressful.
- **Walking on a leash.** Not in the first month. Indoor confidence first.
- **Introducing a second pet.** Wait until your first cat is fully settled (typically 2–3 months).
- **Major furniture changes.** Predictability is calming during the settling-in period.
- **Endless visitors.** Your friends want to meet the cat. Make them wait. The cat is meeting you first.

## Common questions

### My cat won't eat. How long is normal?

Most cats eat within 24 hours. After 48 hours of not eating, call a vet — cats can develop hepatic lipidosis quickly. Try offering a small amount of the same food they had at the shelter, warmed slightly.

### My cat hisses at me. Did I do something wrong?

Almost certainly not. Hissing is a defensive signal — they're saying "I'm not sure about you yet." Patience, predictability, and not pushing for contact will resolve it over days to weeks.

### Should I get a second cat to keep this one company?

Not in the first month. Settle the first cat first. Many cats are perfectly happy solo; adding a second cat without thought makes both cats more stressed.

### How long until my cat is "fully settled"?

Cliché but accurate: 3 days to decompress, 3 weeks to start showing personality, 3 months to feel fully at home. Some cats are faster; some are slower.

### Should I let my cat sleep in my bed?

Personal preference. Pros: bonding, warmth. Cons: 4am zoomies on your face, litter-tracking, allergies. Pick your tradeoffs.

## Next step

Tonight, sit quietly in your cat's space for 15 minutes. Read a book. Don't reach for them. Don't talk much. Just be there. That single repeated habit — quiet presence without demands — is what most cat relationships are built on.
