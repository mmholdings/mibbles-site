A cat who's been confident for years stops jumping on the couch. She watches the door instead of relaxing into the cushion. Her tail flicks. She seems alert in a way she wasn't last month. Something changed.

Cat anxiety is real and surprisingly common — and unlike dog anxiety, it's mostly invisible. Cats withdraw rather than act out. The signs are subtle until they aren't.

<Callout type="quick-answer">
**Common cat anxiety symptoms:** increased hiding, overgrooming (especially belly and inner thighs), litter box avoidance, appetite changes, excessive or unusual vocalization, hypervigilance, aggression toward housemates, and compulsive behaviors. Always rule out medical causes first — many illnesses look like anxiety. Environmental changes, routine, and pheromone products help most cases; severe cases may need a vet behaviorist.
</Callout>

## What feline anxiety looks like

Unlike a panic attack in a human, anxiety in cats tends to be a sustained state of guardedness rather than a sudden episode. The signs:

### Behavioral signs

- **Hiding more than usual** — under the bed, in closets, in spaces they didn't previously use
- **Overgrooming**, often in patches on the belly, inner thighs, or front legs (rule out medical causes first)
- **Litter box avoidance** or going outside the box
- **Decreased appetite** or noticeably anxious eating (gulping, eating only when no one is watching)
- **Aggression toward housemates** — sudden swatting at another cat or person
- **Compulsive behaviors** — pacing, wool-sucking, repetitive licking
- **Loss of interest in play** or favorite activities

### Body language signs

- **Pupils dilated** in normal light
- **Ears swiveling** or pinned back
- **Crouched posture** with weight low
- **Tail tucked tightly** or thrashing
- **Whiskers pulled back**
- **Hypervigilance** — startling easily, scanning the room constantly

### Physical signs (rule out illness first)

- Vomiting or diarrhea with no other cause
- Sudden grooming changes
- Sleeping in unusual places
- Posture changes (hunched, unable to settle)

## The most common triggers

Most cat anxiety traces back to one (or a combination) of these:

### Environmental change

A move, new furniture, renovation, a new household member (human or pet), a child going off to college, even rearranged rooms. Cats are territorial and routine-dependent in ways that surprise many owners.

### Routine disruption

A change in your work schedule, irregular feeding times, the absence of a bonded household member. Cats relax into predictability; chaos is its own stressor.

### Multi-cat tension

The single most underdiagnosed source of household cat anxiety. Two cats can appear to "tolerate" each other while one is chronically stressed by the other's presence. Watch for resource guarding, blocking access to food/litter, or staring contests.

### Under-stimulation

Cats who don't have enough varied input — play, foraging, vertical space, sensory novelty — sometimes develop a generalized restlessness that looks like anxiety.

### Loud noise

Construction, fireworks, a new appliance, a barking neighbor dog. Cats have far better hearing than humans; what's background to you can be deeply stressful to them.

### Medical issues

This is the most important category. Pain (especially arthritis or dental disease), urinary issues, thyroid disease, and many other conditions present as behavior change first. A "anxious cat" is sometimes a cat in pain.

## What helps

Treatment depends on cause. A few approaches help broadly:

### 1. Rule out medical causes

Always step one. If your cat is showing anxiety symptoms, book a vet visit before assuming it's purely behavioral.

### 2. Restore routine

Same meal times, same play times, same quiet hours. Predictability itself is calming for cats.

### 3. Provide choice and escape

Multiple hiding spots, multiple resting areas at different heights, multiple litter boxes in multi-cat households. Cats relax when they have options.

### 4. Pheromone products

Feliway diffusers (plug-in) or sprays use synthetic versions of the facial pheromones cats produce when they feel safe. Reasonable evidence for moderate stress; useful during transitions like moves or new pets.

### 5. Increase enrichment thoughtfully

Not all at once — a stressed cat doesn't want more new things. Slowly add: a second cat tree in a new spot, scheduled wand-toy sessions at consistent times, food puzzles with familiar food, brief Cat TV sessions on calm channels.

### 6. Address multi-cat dynamics

If you have more than one cat and one is anxious, look hard at the dynamic. Provide enough resources (1 box per cat + 1, multiple feeding stations, vertical space). Consider whether the cats need temporary separation and re-introduction.

### 7. Consider veterinary behavior consultation

For persistent or severe anxiety, a board-certified veterinary behaviorist (DACVB) can help. Sometimes the right medication, in combination with environmental work, transforms a cat's quality of life.

## What to avoid

- **Punishing anxiety behaviors.** Hissing at a hiding cat, spraying water at a cat for over-grooming — all make anxiety worse.
- **Forcing interaction.** Pulling an anxious cat out of their hiding spot to "socialize" them deepens the anxiety.
- **Trying every change at once.** Pick one or two interventions and give them 1–2 weeks before adding more. Cats can't process rapid change.
- **Assuming "they'll get over it."** Untreated chronic stress in cats is linked to multiple medical problems.

## Common questions

### How long does it take for anxiety treatment to show results?

Mild anxiety often improves within 1–2 weeks of consistent environmental change. Moderate cases may take 4–8 weeks. Severe cases generally need professional involvement and may take months.

### My cat hides only when guests come over. Is that anxiety?

Probably normal cat behavior, not pathological anxiety. Cats are choosy about new people; many simply prefer to opt out of social situations. As long as they return to normal once guests leave, it's not a concern.

### Will a Thundershirt help an anxious cat?

Some cats find compression calming; many cats actively dislike wearing things. Test in a low-stress moment; don't introduce a new thing during an already stressful situation.

### Are essential oils safe for anxious cats?

No. Many essential oils are toxic to cats — including lavender, eucalyptus, tea tree, peppermint, and citrus. Diffusing them around your cat can cause real harm. Use cat-safe pheromone products instead.
