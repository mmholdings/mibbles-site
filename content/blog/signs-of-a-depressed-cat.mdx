A cat who used to greet you at the door now stays under the bed. The food bowl is still half-full from yesterday. She hasn't groomed her back in a week. Something has changed, and you're trying to figure out whether it's serious.

The thing to know up front: in cats, the behavior pattern we call "depression" usually has either a medical cause or an environmental cause — sometimes both. Either way, it's worth taking seriously, and there's almost always something you can do.

<Callout type="quick-answer">
**The most common signs of a depressed cat** are: hiding or withdrawal, reduced grooming, appetite changes, low energy or excessive sleeping, decreased interest in play or interaction, and vocalization changes. These are real welfare signals — rule out medical causes with your vet first, then address triggers in the environment.
</Callout>

## The signs to watch for

No cat shows all of these. Most show a cluster of three or four. The pattern matters more than any single sign.

### Withdrawal and hiding

A cat who used to sleep on the couch now lives under the bed. They avoid social areas. They flinch from contact they used to seek out. This is one of the most consistent signs of low mood in cats.

### Reduced grooming

Cats are meticulous self-groomers. A scruffy, matted coat — especially on the back and rear, which are harder to reach — is one of the first physical signs that something is off mentally or physically.

### Appetite changes

Either direction matters. Loss of appetite can signal nausea, pain, or stress. Sudden over-eating can signal anxiety. Cats who skip meals for more than 24 hours need a vet check — they can develop hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver) within days of not eating.

### Low energy and excessive sleep

Cats already sleep 12–16 hours a day. *More* sleep than baseline, paired with reduced movement when awake, is a flag. Look at the texture of their wake periods — are they engaged, or just shuffling between spots?

### Loss of interest in play or interaction

The wand toy that used to send them flying now gets a blink. The window where they used to watch birds is ignored. This kind of anhedonia (loss of pleasure in previously enjoyable activities) is one of the clearest behavioral overlaps with human depression.

### Vocalization changes

Some depressed cats become more vocal — extended meowing or yowling, often at night. Others become silent when they used to be chatty. Either change is information.

### Changes in litter box use

Going outside the box, going more often, going less. Always worth a vet visit (urinary issues are common in stressed indoor cats), and a flag for mood as well.

### Aggression toward housemates

A normally social cat who starts swatting at another pet or family member. Often a sign of accumulated stress with no other outlet.

## Rule out medical causes first

This is the most important paragraph in this article. **What looks like depression in a cat is often illness.** Pain, dental disease, thyroid issues, kidney problems, and dozens of other conditions present with withdrawal, hiding, and appetite changes. Cats are evolved to hide weakness. A "depressed cat" who is actually sick can deteriorate quickly if you spend two weeks adding enrichment instead of getting them to a vet.

If you see two or more of the signs above lasting more than 48 hours, book a vet visit. Same week, ideally.

## What commonly triggers low mood in cats

Once you've ruled out (or addressed) medical causes, the usual environmental triggers:

- **Loss of a companion.** The death or departure of a bonded human or animal. Cats grieve — the behavior change can be dramatic and usually improves over a few weeks.
- **A move.** New territory is high stress. Cats often hide for several days and ease back over 2–4 weeks.
- **A new household member.** New baby, new partner, new pet — anything that disrupts the established hierarchy.
- **Routine changes.** A new work schedule, a change in feeding times, less attention than they're used to.
- **Boredom and under-stimulation.** Particularly in indoor cats whose environment hasn't kept up with their needs.
- **Chronic pain.** Arthritis is dramatically under-diagnosed in cats. A "depressed" senior cat is often an arthritic one.

## What helps

The shape of the intervention depends on the cause. A few things help across most situations:

### 1. Maintain (or restore) routine

Cats are routine animals. Consistent feeding times, consistent play times, consistent quiet hours — predictability is itself calming. If a recent change triggered the mood shift, the goal is to rebuild *a* new routine, not to fight the change.

### 2. Add enrichment in small, sustained doses

Five short play sessions a week is dramatically more effective than one heroic 45-minute session. Use a wand toy, food puzzles, a window with passing birds, scent-based enrichment (catnip, silvervine, valerian root). Visual enrichment like cat TV — used in short sessions — slots in here too.

### 3. Restore choice

A common feature of stressed-out indoor cats is a lack of choice — only one resting spot in their preferred temperature, only one food location, only one quiet area. Adding choice (more cat trees, more food locations, more hiding spots, vertical territory) often shifts mood by itself.

### 4. Increase quality interaction

Not more attention — *better* attention. A 10-minute focused play session beats an hour of half-distracted petting. Cats are particular about what counts as connection. Match their preferences.

### 5. Consider pheromones

Feliway and similar synthetic feline pheromones are well-studied. They won't fix a major issue alone, but they often help take the edge off during transitions (move, new pet, household change). Plug-in diffusers are the most convenient delivery.

### 6. Talk to a veterinary behaviorist

If a low mood lasts more than a month after addressing the obvious environmental factors, consider a referral to a board-certified veterinary behaviorist (DACVB). They can assess whether medication might be appropriate. This is real medicine, not a last resort.

## Common questions

### Will a second cat help my lonely cat?

Sometimes — but not always, and adding a cat is a major commitment. Some cats are happier alone. Others bloom with a companion. Talk to a behaviorist before assuming "another cat" is the answer.

### My cat seems depressed after my other cat died. How long should I wait before doing anything?

A few weeks. Many cats work through grief over 2–6 weeks with extra attention, routine, and small enrichment additions. If you're not seeing any improvement after a month, consult your vet.

### Can a depressed cat recover fully?

Yes, in most cases. Mood-related changes — particularly those triggered by an identifiable event — usually improve substantially once the trigger is addressed or time has passed.

### Is medication a last resort?

It's a tool, not a failure. For cats with persistent anxiety or mood issues that don't respond to environmental change, the right medication (prescribed by a vet familiar with feline psychopharmacology) can be life-changing.

## Next step

If you've noticed two or more of the signs in this article lasting more than 48 hours, book a vet visit this week. While you wait, write down the timeline — when did each change start? what else changed in the house around then? That timeline is the single most useful thing you can bring to the appointment.
