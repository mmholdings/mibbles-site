You're at work. You wonder if the cat is okay. You open a phone app, see her sleeping in the sun, and instantly feel better. That's the whole pitch for a cat camera.

What it doesn't have to be: an expensive smart device, a subscription, or video being uploaded to a server in another country.

## What a cat camera is actually for

Three jobs, mostly:

1. **Check in for peace of mind.** Is the cat fine? Most days yes. Knowing that costs you 10 seconds and changes your workday.
2. **Notice patterns.** Is your cat sleeping all day or pacing? Does the new dog next door bark and stress them out? Camera footage answers questions you couldn't otherwise.
3. **Two-way audio for connection.** Saying hi from across town. Effects vary by cat (some cats find it calming, others confused).

What a cat camera is *not* good for: live entertainment for your cat. They don't watch a screen of you. They want their snack at 5pm.

## If you're buying a dedicated camera

If you don't have a spare iPhone, here's what to look for:

### Camera quality

1080p minimum. 2K is nicer but not required. Low-light/night vision matters — your cat is active at dawn and dusk.

### Field of view

130° or wider lets one camera cover most of a room. Narrower means more cameras.

### Two-way audio

Useful for some cats; ignore for others. Worth having; rarely the reason to choose one camera over another.

### Motion alerts you can actually tune

Most pet cameras over-alert by default. You want adjustable sensitivity, motion zones, and the ability to mute alerts during quiet hours.

### Local storage option

Many cameras only offer cloud storage with a subscription. Look for SD-card support or an on-device option — you'll save money and your video won't sit on someone's server.

### Privacy policy you can stomach

Read the actual policy. Some cameras (especially budget ones from manufacturers based overseas) have privacy practices that surprise people. Wyze, Eufy, TP-Link Tapo, and Logitech are typically more transparent than nameless Amazon options.

## Cameras to skip

### Smart treat dispensers

The dispensing part is fun for the first three days. Most cats either ignore it, learn to game it, or get frustrated by it. The camera quality is usually mediocre. Spend the money on a cat tree.

### Anything with a built-in "laser"

Cats can develop frustration with lasers because they can never catch the dot. As a workday entertainment tool, they often do more harm than good.

### Cameras that require cloud accounts to view live

You should be able to see live video from your phone without paying anything extra. If a camera gates the live feed behind a subscription, walk away.

## Common questions

### What's the best free cat cam app?

Most free cam apps either ad-spam you or stream to a third party's servers. For a free option that just works, try Manything; for a paid, local-only option built specifically for cats, Mibbles.

### Can I record video for later viewing?

Some camera apps support local recording; some only show live. Decide what you actually need — most owners watch live for 30 seconds and never re-watch.

### What about multiple cameras?

If you have a multi-room setup, two cheap cameras (Wyze or TP-Link Tapo) often beat one expensive one. Just be sure they support local recording or you'll be paying a subscription per device.

### Will my cat care that I'm watching?

No. Most cats don't notice the camera exists.
