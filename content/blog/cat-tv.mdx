Your cat is staring at the laptop again. Not at you — at the screensaver. Maybe the fish move slowly enough that something old in her brain turns on. You wonder if it counts as enrichment, or if it's just the indoor equivalent of doomscrolling.

It actually counts. Cat TV — purpose-built video and audio for cats — is one of the more researched corners of feline enrichment. The catch is that "leave the TV on all day" is not how you use it well.

<Callout type="quick-answer">
**Cat TV** is video and audio content designed for cats — usually slow footage of birds, fish, or other prey. Used in 20–60 minute sessions, it provides visual enrichment that indoor cats are otherwise missing. It works because it engages the same predator-detection circuits cats use when watching real prey through a window.
</Callout>

## What "cat TV" actually means

Cat TV is a loose category that covers anything filmed or designed to hold a cat's visual attention: bird-feeder cams, fish tanks, squirrels foraging, slow nature scenes. In the last few years it's grown into a full content category — YouTube channels with millions of subscribers, dedicated apps, and even Apple TV screensavers people repurpose for their cats.

The core idea is simple. Indoor cats don't have access to the constant low-grade visual stimuli a free-roaming cat would — birds passing the window, squirrels in the yard, the slow drift of fish in a pond. Replicating those scenes on a screen gives them something to track, react to, and process. It's not a replacement for play. It's a supplement.

## Why cats engage with screens at all

Cats have a higher flicker-fusion threshold than humans — the rate at which a flickering image appears as continuous motion. Older CRT and low-refresh-rate displays often looked choppy to cats. Modern OLED screens (which is most TVs and iPads sold in the last few years) refresh fast enough that the image looks smooth. That's part of why cat TV took off when it did.

Cats also have excellent motion detection — better than humans for movement in low-light, peripheral vision — and a strong evolved response to small prey-shaped motion. A bird hopping across a feeder triggers something deep. They will watch.

What cats are weaker at:

- **Color discrimination.** Cats are dichromats — they see in shades of blue and green-yellow. Red looks brown-ish.
- **Sharp resolution on small objects.** They can't make out fine detail the way you can.
- **Sustained focus on still images.** A photograph won't hold a cat's attention. Movement will.

This is why effective cat TV looks the way it does: slow, deliberate, mid-frame movement, with simple color palettes and good contrast.

## What cats actually like to watch

There's a hierarchy. Across most cats, attention rankings tend to look roughly like this:

1. **Birds** — especially small songbirds at a feeder, with realistic chirping audio
2. **Squirrels and small rodents** — high-engagement, often gets stalking behavior
3. **Fish** — calmer, often used as background or wind-down
4. **Insects** — moths, butterflies — surprisingly engaging
5. **Cats** — yes, some cats love watching other cats; many ignore them entirely

Things that don't work as well: cluttered nature documentaries with lots of cuts, anything with quick zooms, video with loud music. Cats are easily disengaged by overstimulation.

## How to use cat TV without overdoing it

This is where most owners go wrong. More is not better.

### Keep sessions short

The general guidance from feline enrichment specialists is to run cat TV in **20–60 minute sessions, a few times a day**. The 9-hour "while I'm at work" stream is not what we want. Cats need variety — moments of intense focus followed by rest. A screen that's always on becomes wallpaper.

### Watch your cat's signals

- **Engaged:** soft staring, slow head tracking, occasional chirping
- **Predatory but happy:** crouch position, slow tail twitch, paw raise
- **Overstimulated and frustrated:** dilated pupils, fast tail lashing, pawing at the screen, growling

If you see frustration, end the session and offer something they *can* catch — a wand toy, a food puzzle, a feathered kicker. Frustrated prey drive with nowhere to go is the failure mode of cat TV.

### Pair it with real play

Cat TV is most useful when it bookends or supplements play, not when it replaces it. A common rhythm: 15 minutes of wand play → 10 minutes on a bird feeder channel → quiet rest. Predatory sequence completed.

### Mount the device safely

Cats will paw at screens. iPads on flat surfaces get knocked. Either wall-mount the device, set it behind a barrier, or accept that the screen will get scratched. Bigger TVs are usually fine because cats can't reach them.

## Common questions

### Will my cat get addicted to cat TV?

No. Cats don't develop screen dependence the way humans can. They disengage when they're tired of it. The bigger risk is owners over-relying on it as enrichment and skipping interactive play.

### Can I leave cat TV on all day while I'm at work?

We don't recommend it. Long passive sessions can lead to frustration if your cat is in a high prey-drive mode with nowhere to release it. If you want background activity for a cat who's home alone, try shorter scheduled sessions (one in the morning, one mid-afternoon), and pair them with a food puzzle.

### What if my cat ignores cat TV completely?

Some cats — older, low-drive, or just not visually oriented — won't engage. That's fine. Try a different format of enrichment: scent (catnip, silvervine, valerian), texture (sisal posts, paper bags), or food puzzles. There's no single right answer for every cat.

### Does cat TV work for kittens?

Yes, often very well — kittens have huge novelty drive. Keep their sessions even shorter (10–15 minutes) and supervise closely; their impulse control is poor and they're more likely to leap at a screen.

### Should the audio be on?

Yes, in most cases. The chirps, rustles, and water sounds are doing real work — cats use audio to localize prey just as much as vision. Keep the volume gentle, not full-blast.
