Eight hours. You're at the office, on a job site, or just deep in back-to-back meetings. The cat has the apartment to himself. The default plan for most owners is "extra food and the TV on" — and it's almost always too little of one thing and too much of another.

Here's a better playbook.

<Callout type="quick-answer">
**To entertain a cat while you're at work**, set up a rotation of small, low-effort activities that fire at different times: a food puzzle at morning departure, a short cat TV session midday (auto-stop), window access with bird activity, hiding spots and vertical territory, and a quality play session the moment you walk in. The goal is variety across the day — not one big thing.
</Callout>

## What cats actually want from a workday

Cats are crepuscular — most active at dawn and dusk. The middle of your workday is also the middle of their natural rest window. That's the good news: an 8-hour stretch where they sleep most of the time is biologically normal.

What goes wrong isn't the sleep. It's:

- **No enrichment options when they do wake** (often after 1–2pm)
- **The food bowl as the only interesting thing in the room**
- **Pent-up energy that explodes at 6:30pm**

Solving for those three is the whole job.

## The workday cat playbook

### Before you leave (5 minutes)

1. **Short wand-toy play** (5 minutes). Don't skip this. A brief morning play session takes the edge off and starts the day with a successful "hunt."
2. **Hide a puzzle feeder** with breakfast or a portion of the day's food. Start easy if your cat is new to foraging.
3. **Open the curtains** to a window with bird activity if you have one.

### Mid-morning to early afternoon (set up to run automatically)

4. **Scheduled cat TV session**, 20–30 minutes around 11am or noon. This is a Mibbles superpower — schedule a session with Siri shortcuts and an auto-stop timer. You don't need it running all day; you need one session of engaged attention during their wake window.
5. **A second food puzzle** in a different room. If you can hide it before you leave with a timed pet feeder, even better — but a simple second puzzle, placed where they'll find it on their afternoon patrol, works too.

### The environment is doing work all day

6. **Multiple cozy spots** in different temperatures and lighting (a sunny window perch, a covered cardboard box in a closet, a high shelf).
7. **Vertical territory.** Cat trees, wall shelves, or a single window perch transforms a room from cat-poor to cat-rich.
8. **Rotating scents.** Leave out something new once or twice a week — a fresh piece of catnip, a new toy with silvervine, a paper bag from yesterday's groceries.

### The moment you walk in

9. **10–15 minutes of interactive play** before food. This is the single most important habit. Your cat has been waiting all day. Wand toy. Real prey movement. Let them catch it.
10. **Then dinner.** Hunt → catch → kill → eat. You've completed the natural sequence, and your evening starts calm.

## What to avoid

### Leaving cat TV on for the whole 8 hours

Discussed at length in [our piece on cat TV session length](/blog/how-long-should-cats-watch-cat-tv). Long passive prey content can build frustration with no release. A scheduled 30-minute session beats a 9-hour stream every time.

### Free-feeding to "keep them occupied"

Removes one of the easiest sources of enrichment — the meal itself. Most adult cats do better with 2–4 scheduled portions, ideally at least one in a puzzle.

### Skipping morning play "because I'm in a rush"

The morning session is the cheapest fix in your toolkit. Five minutes. Doesn't have to be elaborate. Just real prey-style movement and a successful catch.

### Adding a new cat without doing the introduction work

A compatible second cat can transform your cat's workday. An incompatible one will make both cats less happy. Slow intros over 2–3 weeks; don't skip the protocol.

## Special cases

### Kittens under 6 months

Don't leave a kitten alone for full workdays. They need feeding, attention, and supervision more often. A midday cat sitter, a kitten-safe room, or working from home are the realistic options.

### Senior cats

Often better adjusted to long quiet days but may have specific medical needs (regular meds, joint comfort). Talk to your vet about whether an 8-hour stretch is appropriate for your particular senior.

### Newly adopted cats (first 2–4 weeks)

Don't leave a brand-new cat alone for full days right away. Build up gradually as they settle into the new environment.

### Cats with separation anxiety

Real but under-diagnosed in cats. Signs include excessive vocalization on departure, destructive behavior, litter box accidents, or extreme greeting behavior. Talk to a vet or veterinary behaviorist — sometimes medication is appropriate.

## Common questions

### Should I get a pet camera?

A pet camera (or a dedicated old iPhone running Cat Cam) is genuinely useful — for peace of mind and for noticing patterns. Most owners report sleeping better at work once they have one.

### How do I tell if my cat is unhappy during the workday?

Use a camera for a week. Look for: how much time spent moving versus sleeping, signs of pacing or vocalization, whether they engage with the puzzles you left, where they choose to rest. The footage tells you more than any list of "signs" can.

### Can I leave the food out and let them graze?

You can, but you're trading away one of your easiest enrichment levers. Portioned meals in puzzle feeders do more enrichment work than the same calories from a free-feed bowl.

### What if I work 12-hour shifts?

Genuinely worth getting help. A neighbor doing a 10-minute check-in or a part-time cat sitter changes the picture significantly. Cats are independent — but they're not indifferent.
